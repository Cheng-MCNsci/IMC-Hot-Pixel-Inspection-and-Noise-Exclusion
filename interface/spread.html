1. Choose an aggregate to inspect

A hot-pixel core can be surrounded by weaker contamination. That surrounding signal can overlap plausible biological intensities. Inspect its spatial pattern before deciding whether a spread mask is justified. Small aggregates can also need spread review.

2. Draw a comparison region and check the settings

Choose nearby, comparable tissue that looks free of this deposit’s spread. Click polygon corners on an image, or drag a freehand boundary, then finish the drawing. You can add several regions. All selected acquired pixels count, including cell structures and zeros. Avoid the hot core and obvious contamination; the viewer reports accidental core overlap.

No reference region drawn. Automatic spread prediction requires an adequate reference.

No prediction mask? Check the bright-pixel threshold

The default bright-pixel threshold is 75% of the marker’s hot-pixel intensity cutoff. This is an adjustable starting value. Weaker noise spread can have intensities similar to biological signal, so the default may be too high to detect it.

  1. First check that your drawn reference has enough qualifying patches.
  2. If no orange prediction mask appears, lower the bright-pixel threshold toward, or below, the intensity range of plausible biological signal seen in the raw marker values. Keep the hot-core cutoff unchanged.
  3. Click Update analysis, then inspect the directional results and proposed mask against the tissue structures. Adjust further if needed; do not lower the threshold simply to force a mask.

Screen brightness is different: a pixel can look white at a low display maximum while staying below the bright-pixel threshold. Use raw hover values and intensity bins to choose the threshold. A missing mask does not prove the image is clean. Saved reviews retain their applied settings.

Advanced prediction settings

There are 16 clockwise wedges, starting at the top. Distances run from a sampled pixel’s center to the nearest core-pixel edge, using the supplied X/Y calibration. Reference patch ranges describe local variation; they are not confidence intervals. Two later consecutive excess rings invalidate an earlier endpoint. Nonzero density is a supporting measurement, not a veto.

How a candidate mask is predicted · plain-language explanation
  1. Measure the percentage of bright pixels in small patches of your drawn reference.
  2. Look near the core in each direction. If bright pixels are unusually common there, investigate that direction.
  3. Follow the direction outward. Look for two adequately sampled rings in a row that fit the reference range and are less bright than the near-core region.
  4. Propose an endpoint at the beginning of those rings, unless a later sustained excess contradicts it.
  5. Shade all acquired pixels between the core and that endpoint. This includes weak pixels. Review the proposal against tissue structures and add missed areas manually if warranted.

No near-core excess means this rule did not detect spread; it does not prove absence. A missing reference or unresolved endpoint remains explicitly unfinished.

5. Record your interpretation and export

Drawings and applied settings autosave; notes and decisions save on leaving the field or pressing Save review. Changing analysis inputs resets the review decision. Backups contain settings, source-coordinate drawings and manual pixels. Mask exports contain automatic, manual, combined candidate, accepted, unresolved and core pixels separately. All-saved export includes saved entries only, not every aggregate in the dataset. No cells or image pixels are changed.
